You are making an animated 2D character for a side-view browser game, where small
pixel-art characters walk, run, jump, fall and attack each other.

I will send you one or more reference images of a character. They may be pixel art,
drawings or real photos. Your job is to turn the reference into a 2D pixel-art-style
game character and produce every animation frame the game needs, filling in all the
poses the references don't show. It does not have to be on a strict pixel grid; what
matters is that it reads as pixel art, that it MOVES like pixel art, and that every
frame follows the rules below.

WHAT TO DELIVER
1. A folder named after the character (lowercase, no spaces), delivered as a zip,
   containing exactly these seven files. Each is a single horizontal row of
   equal-size cells, left to right in playback order, PNG with a transparent
   background:

  file           min frames  how long it lasts     loops  what it shows
  idle.png           8       0.8-1.6 s per cycle   yes    standing, breathing; small motion only
  walk.png           8       0.7-1.0 s per cycle   yes    one full walk cycle (both steps)
  run.png            8       0.5-0.8 s per cycle   yes    one full run cycle, body leaning forward
  jump.png           8       0.3-0.5 s             no     crouch, push off, rise; last frame is held while going up
  fall.png           8       exactly 0.33 s        no     turning from rising to falling; last frame is held
  fall_loop.png      8       0.3-0.6 s per cycle   yes    falling, small flutter of limbs/cloth/hair
  attack.png        18       at most 0.45 s        no     one attack forward, then recover to the idle pose

   The frame counts are minimums, not targets: no animation has fewer than 8
   frames, and the attack has at least 18. For each animation, decide how many
   frames this character needs (at most 16, and at most 24 for the attack):
   - start from the minimum and add frames where the motion would otherwise jump:
     a heavy or large body, four legs, a long weapon arc, a two-part attack, or
     cloth, hair, a tail or ears that follow through after the body stops;
   - every frame must be a real step of the motion: do not reach the minimum by
     repeating a frame or by nudging one pixel. If an animation feels done in
     fewer frames, add anticipation, overshoot, settle and follow-through;
   - more frames make the motion smoother, not longer: the duration stays inside
     the range in the table, and fps = frames / duration (any value up to 60,
     decimals allowed). An 18-frame attack in 0.45 s plays at 40 fps;
   - write down, per animation, the count you chose and one line of why.

   The attack: if the character has a weapon, it attacks WITH that weapon (a sword
   swings or thrusts, a gun fires, a staff strikes). With no weapon, it punches,
   kicks, shoves, bites or headbutts, whatever suits the character. Build it as
   anticipation (4-6 frames: wind-up, weight shift), strike (4-6 frames: the
   weapon or limb travelling, contact, the widest pose), recovery (the rest:
   overshoot, settle, back to the idle pose).

   The attack may leave the cell. attack.png, and only attack.png, may use its own
   larger cell (wider, taller, or both, up to three times the normal cell width)
   so a long weapon, a lunge, a projectile leaving a gun or a jumping strike has
   room. The character still stands on the same ground line, and its feet in the
   first and last attack frames are where they are in the idle. Every attack frame
   uses that same larger cell.

   Also put a "sheet.json" in the folder with: the cell width and height, the y of
   the ground line in the cell, the x of the point between the feet in the idle,
   "size" (the character's standing height as a multiple of the reference human,
   see SIZE AND PROPORTIONS), "standing_height_px" (its height in pixels in the
   first idle frame, from the top of the head to the ground line), and for each
   animation its file, frames, fps and loop, exactly as you drew it. If the attack
   uses a larger cell, add under "attack": "cell_width", "cell_height", "ground_y"
   and "feet_x" (the same two measures, taken in the attack cell).

2. A preview page (see PREVIEW PAGE below), so I can judge the animations before
   importing.

The durations are hard limits: the game cuts to the next state after them. The
whole attack must fit in 0.45 s and the whole fall in 0.33 s, however many frames
you use, so set their fps from the frame count. The game plays each sheet at the
frames and fps you write in sheet.json.

SIZE AND PROPORTIONS
Every character in the game stands next to the others, so size is part of the
design. The game does not resize your art to fit: it draws every character at the
same pixel density, so the size you draw IS the size in the game.

- The reference is an ordinary adult human, standing: exactly 128 pixels from the
  top of the head to the ground line. That is size 1.0.
- Decide your character's size as a multiple of that, measured the same way
  (top of the head or ears to the ground, standing in the idle), and draw it at
  that many pixels: size x 128.

    what it is                               size        pixels tall
    small animal (cat, small dog, bird)      0.20-0.30   26-38    below the knee
    dog, wolf, big animal on four legs       0.30-0.45   38-58    knee to mid-thigh
    child, goblin, small creature            0.55-0.75   70-96    waist to chest
    ordinary adult                           1.00        128
    tall or heavy fighter                    1.10-1.25   141-160  a head taller
    giant, ogre, mech                        1.30-1.50   166-192  the maximum

  The knee of the reference human is at 0.28, the hip at 0.50, the chest at 0.75.
- Width follows from the real proportions of the character at that size; never
  squash or stretch it to fit a cell. A wide body (a beast, a rider on a mount,
  someone in heavy armour) is simply wider. For a rider, the rider keeps human
  proportions (the rider alone would be size 1.0) and the pair is taller and wider
  as it really would be. Keep the widest standing pose under 1.6 x 128 pixels.
- Heads, hands, feet and weapons keep the proportions of the reference image. Do
  not enlarge the head to make a small character readable; a small character is
  small.
- Choose the cell size AFTER the size: big enough for the widest pose, with a few
  pixels of margin.
- Do not ask me for the size. Propose it yourself from what the character is and
  state it with the designs in the first step; I will correct it if I disagree.

HOW TO ANIMATE (read this before drawing anything)
This is frame-by-frame pixel-art animation, not an image that gets warped.

1. Study the references. Look at every image I sent and write down what the
   character is made of: head, hair or helmet, torso, each arm, each hand, each
   leg, each foot, weapon or carried item, cloth, tail, ears, anything that hangs
   or trails. Note each part's colours, its size relative to the head, and where it
   attaches. This parts list is your model sheet; keep it and reuse it.
2. Research how THIS character moves. If the character is a known one (from a
   game, a comic, a show, a film) or a real person, search online for how they
   actually move before you animate: their stance, how they walk and run, how
   they jump, and above all how they attack. Look for the signature move: the
   weapon they use and how they hold it, a named technique, a companion or power
   that appears with them, a catchphrase pose. The attack should be recognisably
   theirs, not a generic punch. Note what you found, with the sources, in two or
   three lines per animation. If the character is original, decide these from
   the design instead.
3. Research the motion itself. Before each animation, search online for
   pixel-art references of that kind of motion (walk cycles, run cycles, jump
   arcs, falling poses, attack anticipation and recovery, and the same for
   four-legged, floating or unusual bodies if that is what the character is).
   Look at how many key poses they use and how each body part moves between them.
   Do not copy anyone's art; learn the poses and the timing.
4. Ask me when it is a matter of taste. If the research gives more than one good
   option (two signature attacks, a weapon or bare hands, with or without the
   companion, a calm or an aggressive idle), or if you are unsure the motion
   suits the character, show me the options in a sentence each, say which you
   would pick and why, and ask. Ask once, with all your questions together,
   before drawing the animation; do not ask about things the rules already
   decide.
5. Plan key poses first. For each animation, decide the key poses (for a walk:
   contact, down, passing, up, for each leg) and only then the frames between.
6. Draw the next frame from the previous one, part by part. For every new frame,
   go through the parts list: where is the head now, the torso, each arm, each
   hand, each leg, each foot, the weapon, the cloth? Move each part on purpose.
   Check the frame against the one before it and the one after it.
7. Analyse every frame you produce. After drawing a frame, look at it again and
   identify each body part in it. If a part is missing, duplicated, changed size,
   changed colour or detached, redraw the frame.
8. Animate like pixel art:
   - pose to pose, with clear readable silhouettes; hold a strong pose rather than
     adding mushy in-betweens;
   - parts move in whole steps; no blur, no smears, no morphing or melting between
     frames, no ghost trails;
   - the same clusters of pixels and the same outline thickness in every frame;
   - small motion (breathing, cloth, hair) is a shift of a few pixels, not a
     redraw of the whole character;
   - the character must look hand-animated at a low frame count, not like a video
     that was pixelated afterwards.
9. Use subagents when it helps. For example: one subagent per animation, each
   given the approved design frame and the parts list; one to research references
   for a motion; and one reviewer that checks every sheet against the rules and
   the parts list and reports the frames that break them. You stay responsible for
   consistency between sheets.

RULES FOR EVERY FRAME
1. Side view, facing RIGHT. The game mirrors the sprite for left.
2. One cell size for the six sheets other than the attack, chosen to fit the
   character at its size (see SIZE AND PROPORTIONS), identical everywhere. The
   attack may have its own larger cell.
3. Same character, same scale, same proportions, same colours and same outfit in
   every frame. No drifting details between frames or between sheets.
4. Feet on the same ground line in every cell, and the body at the same horizontal
   position in the cell. In idle, walk, run and attack at least one foot touches
   that line in every frame. In jump, fall and fall_loop the character is in the
   air but stays at the same horizontal position; do not move it up or down the
   cell to show height, the game does that.
5. The character animates in place. Walk and run cycles do not travel across the cell.
6. Loops must be seamless: the last frame leads back into the first, and is not a
   copy of the first.
7. Fully transparent background. No ground, no drop shadow, no glow, no outline
   halo, no motion blur, no dust. The game adds its own shadow. The attack may show
   the weapon's own effect (a muzzle flash, a short blade arc) only if it is
   compact, hard-edged and gone by the recovery frames.
8. Hard edges: every pixel is either fully opaque or fully transparent.
9. Keep poses compact in every animation except the attack: nothing should swing
   far outside the body in idle, walk, run, jump or fall. The attack is the
   exception (see its larger cell above).
10. If the character carries something (a weapon, a bag), it stays in every
    animation, held the same way, and it is what the attack uses.
11. Limited palette with clear shading steps, readable at small size on a dark
    background. Give the head a clear silhouette; a label floats above it.

STYLE REFERENCE
Match the feel of a classic 16-bit side-scroller hero: about 3 to 4 heads tall,
strong silhouette, a few shading steps per colour, readable face. Idle is calm
(breathing, cloth or hair moving slightly). Walk is relaxed, run is clearly faster
with a forward lean and bigger arm swing, jump has a visible crouch before take-off,
fall has limbs and cloth trailing upward, the attack snaps out and settles back.

The sprite sheets are embedded as PNG data URIs: the page fetches no image files.
It must have every one of these features:

- Header: the character's name, one line describing the motion study, and four

  "0.40 x human").
- A size check: the idle frame standing on the same ground line as a flat grey

- A sticky toolbar that controls everything on the page at once:

  - Step frame (pauses and advances every animation by one frame);
  - Restart (all animations and the combined sequence back to frame 1);
  - Checkerboard toggle (light checkerboard behind the sprites instead of the dark

  - Ground guides toggle (a dashed line at the ground y, and a small cross at the
    feet x, drawn over every canvas);
  - Flip left toggle (mirror the character horizontally);
  - Zoom slider from 1x to 8x in whole steps, with the current value shown;
  - a status readout: PLAYING or PAUSED.
- Combined sequence: ONE canvas where a single character plays idle, walk, run,
  jump, fall, fall_loop (twice) and attack in order, then repeats. Next to it:
  the current and next state as a title, a row of seven pills with the current
  state highlighted, a progress bar across the whole sequence, and a line with
  the frame number of the current state (Frame 03 / 10), its fps, and the elapsed
  time over the total (1.2 s / 4.4 s).
- Individual animations: a grid of seven cards, all playing at the same time,
  each at its own fps. Each card shows the name, "N frames · M fps", the canvas,
  a live frame counter (Frame 03 / 10), and whether it loops. Looping ones play
  forever; one-shot ones (jump, fall, attack) play once, hold the last frame for
  one second, then restart.
- If the attack has a larger cell, its canvas is that size and the character's
  feet line up with the other animations (same ground line on screen); in the
  combined sequence the canvas is large enough for the attack and the other
  states are drawn at the same feet position, so nothing jumps when it switches.
- Canvases draw with nearest-neighbour scaling (image-rendering: pixelated, image
  smoothing off), on a dark background (#1a1028) unless the checkerboard is on.
- Footer: a note that the PNGs are embedded and nothing is fetched, the ground y,
  the idle feet x, and confirmation that alpha is binary.
- It works at phone width: the grid collapses to one column and the toolbar wraps.
